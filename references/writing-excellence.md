# Writing excellence

Contents: 1 why slop happens, 2 pattern sweep, 3 how to write well instead, 4 revision procedure, 5 cautions.

## 1. Why slop happens
Language models drift toward the most statistically likely phrasing. Wikipedia editors who catalogued thousands of AI drafts describe this as regression to the mean: specific facts get smoothed into generic statements that fit many topics. The patterns below are signs, not proof. Several together matter; one does not. Fixing surface tells without fixing the thin content underneath only hides the problem.

## 2. Pattern sweep (look for these, then fix the cause)
- **Puffery and significance claims**: "stands as", "serves as a testament", "pivotal", "rich tapestry", "key turning point", "underscores its importance". Fix: state the fact; delete the claim of importance.
- **Tacked-on analysis**: trailing participle clauses ("...highlighting its lasting influence", "...reflecting broader trends"). Fix: cut, or replace with a real, sourced consequence.
- **Negative parallelism**: "It's not just X, it's Y." "Not only... but also..." Fix: say Y.
- **Rule of three**: reflexive triplets of adjectives, benefits or takeaways. Fix: use the true number of items.
- **Stock vocabulary**: delve, intricate, tapestry, landscape, foster, enhance, crucial, vibrant, seamless, robust, leverage, unlock, elevate, journey, navigate (figurative). Fix: use the plain verb or noun.
- **Compulsive framing**: generic openers ("In today's fast-paced world"), "In conclusion", "Overall", closing paragraphs that restate the text. Fix: start with the point; end when finished.
- **"Challenges and future outlook"** sections with vague optimism. Fix: name the actual problem or drop the section.
- **Artificial balance**: "While X, it's important to also consider Y" on every claim. Fix: take the position the evidence supports, note real uncertainty once.
- **Empty sophistication**: long sentences that say little. Fix: reduce to the claim; if nothing remains, delete.
- **Formatting overkill**: bold on every key term, emoji in headings, bullets for what should be prose, headings on a 200-word answer.
- **Punctuation habits**: heavy em dashes, colon-led reveals ("The result: ..."), semicolon chains. Moderation is fine; a pattern of one per sentence is a tell.
- **Uniform rhythm**: every paragraph 3-4 sentences, similar length, same shape (claim, support, mini-summary).
- **Assistant residue**: "Here's a...", "I hope this helps", "Certainly!", knowledge-cutoff hedges, leftover placeholders.
- **Source theatre**: sentences that announce sources without adding content ("according to independent coverage").

## 3. How to write well instead
- Know the one thing the reader should take away. Put it first.
- Prefer concrete: a name, a number the user gave, a place, an example, a comparison from the subject's own world.
- Use strong, plain verbs; cut adverbs and hedges unless the uncertainty is real.
- Vary sentence length on purpose. A short sentence after a long one lands. Do not do this mechanically.
- Let paragraphs differ in size because ideas differ in size.
- Voice: choose a register (dry, warm, urgent, curious) from audience and purpose and hold it. Voice is selection and rhythm, not slang or quirks.
- Depth without pretension: define terms once, then use them. Keep the sophisticated word only if it is the most exact one.
- Keep facts exact. Never invent sources, statistics, quotes or achievements. Mark gaps as [needs source].
- For academic text: precise claims, hedging calibrated to evidence, methods and limits stated, terms used consistently, no inflated novelty claims.
- For children's or student writing: keep the author's own voice and level; improve clarity, not sophistication.

## 4. Revision procedure
1. Summarise the piece in one sentence. If you cannot, restructure before editing sentences.
2. Cut: delete every sentence that restates, announces, or praises.
3. Concretise: mark each abstraction, replace it with a detail or remove it.
4. Sweep section 2 patterns; fix by rewriting, not by synonym swapping.
5. Read aloud for rhythm; break any run of same-shaped sentences.
6. Check facts against what the user supplied.
7. Check the opening and the ending: the first sentence carries news; the last adds, not repeats.

## 5. Cautions
- Do not mechanically ban words or punctuation; a ban just moves the pattern elsewhere.
- Humans use these patterns too. Judge by density and by whether specifics are missing.
- AI detectors are unreliable; never optimise for them or claim a text is "undetectable".
- Do not deceive about authorship where disclosure is required (school work, journals, competitions). Offer help that improves the student's own writing instead.
