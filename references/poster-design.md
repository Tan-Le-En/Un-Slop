# Posters, infographics, charts

Contents: 1 why amateur, 2 structure, 3 layout, 4 text, 5 imagery, 6 data, 7 science and competition posters, 8 exceptions.

## 1. Why AI-made posters look amateur
No focal point; everything competes. Too much text. Decorative graphics unrelated to content. Mixed illustration and icon styles. Inconsistent margins and alignment. Rounded boxes around everything. Shadows with no light logic. Weak colour relationships (many similar saturations). Charts with ornament. Fabricated numbers.

## 2. Structure
1. One message, one sentence.
2. Primary focal point (a headline, number, image or diagram), secondary (supporting evidence), tertiary (details, credits).
3. Reading path: choose Z, F, column or centre-out and arrange content along it.
4. Cut. Posters are read at a glance first, close up second.

## 3. Layout
- Build a grid (columns plus margins plus baseline). Swiss and International Typographic style relies on grid, asymmetry, flush-left ragged-right type and strong scale contrast; it is a toolkit, not a mandate.
- Margins equal and generous; outer margin at least the gutter width times two.
- Group by proximity; divide with space before lines, lines before boxes.
- Scale contrast: focal element at least 3x the size of the next level.
- Limit palette to a ground, an ink and one or two accents. Use tints for hierarchy.
- Align edges; let one element break the grid deliberately.

## 4. Text
- Word budget: title under 10 words; panels under 50; whole poster commonly 300-800 words for research, far fewer for event posters.
- Active headlines that state findings. Short captions. Bulleted fragments only for parallel items.
- Body type readable from about 1.5 m on research posters (24pt or larger), headings 36pt or larger, title 72pt or larger on A0 as a starting point.

## 5. Imagery
- One image style per piece (photo, flat vector, line, diagram). Same stroke weight, same corner logic, same palette.
- Use real photos or the user's own artwork when available. Check resolution at print size (300 dpi at final size, 150 minimum for large format).
- Icons only where they carry meaning; label them.

## 6. Data visualisation
- Choose chart by question: comparison (bars), change over time (lines), part-to-whole (few categories, bars or a single stacked bar), relationship (scatter), distribution (histogram, dot plot).
- Maximise data-ink (Tufte): remove 3D, heavy gridlines, boxes, gradients, redundant legends. Label directly. Start bars at zero. Sort bars by value unless order is meaningful.
- Colour: grey for context, one colour to highlight the point of the chart. Colour-blind safe palettes (Okabe-Ito). Do not encode meaning in colour alone. Contrast 3:1 for non-text marks.
- Title states the takeaway. Annotate the one thing that matters. Source and units always.
- Never invent or smooth data. If numbers are missing, leave a labelled placeholder.

## 7. Science and competition posters
- Follow the competition's rules first (size, orientation, language, sections, judging rubric).
- Typical flow: question or problem, method or design, evidence or demo, result, meaning, next step. Make the problem and the result most prominent.
- Show the actual prototype, circuit, data or experiment, labelled. Judges reward evidence of real work over decoration.
- Credit data and images. For multilingual displays, keep a consistent hierarchy across languages and check fonts cover all scripts.

## 8. Exceptions
Maximalist, collage, psychedelic and festival posters can be excellent. They still need a dominant element, a limited type system and an alignment logic. Rounded boxes and shadows are fine in a playful system when consistent.
